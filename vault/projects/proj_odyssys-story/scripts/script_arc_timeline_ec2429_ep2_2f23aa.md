## [00:30] Section 2: Why Surface Accuracy Fails
- **Type**: `problem_breakdown` | **Target**: 67s | **Words**: 161

> [!tip] Visual Anchor
> [DIAGRAM: Surface Accuracy vs Structural Timeline: costume details vs cause-and-effect dependencies]

Most history-driven films fail because they treat accuracy as a costume department problem. You get the right helmet, the right banner, the right dialect, and then the scene still breaks. The real failure is timeline logic. A character knows something they could not know. A siege happens before the walls are built. A messenger arrives faster than a horse can move. In production, this shows up as flat exposition, because the writer is patching holes with dialogue instead of fixing cause and effect. Christopher Nolan's comments about history accuracy push the same point: the audience may not catch every anachronism, but they feel when the world is not coherent. If the facts are decorative, the story becomes a museum diorama. If the facts are load-bearing, the drama gets stronger. The trap is that directors, editors, and consultants often argue about surface details while the structural timeline is still loose. That is why a film can look authentic and still feel false.

## [01:37] Section 3: The Proximity Test
- **Type**: `deep_dive` | **Target**: 163s | **Words**: 393

> [!tip] Visual Anchor
> [CODE: timeline_ledger: scene | date | fact_type | source | risk | fix | proximity: inside/adjacent/outside]

Here is the practical model. Treat historical accuracy like a system architecture, not a checklist. The general engineering principle is simple: define constraints, map dependencies, and keep one source of truth. You need three layers. First, hard facts: dates, geography, technology, law, religion, and material limits. These are the constraints that cannot be broken without a visible cost. If a city has no port, your trade route cannot depend on ships. If a weapon does not exist yet, your battle cannot use it. Second, soft facts: customs, speech patterns, social hierarchy, and daily life. These can bend, but they must bend consistently. A king may be rude, but the court should still understand why that rudeness matters. Third, narrative facts: the story's emotional logic. This is where Nolan's point lands. A film can be close to historic facts and still feel false if the characters make choices that no human in that world would make. The fix is a timeline ledger. For every scene, record the date, location, who knows what, what has changed, and what the next scene depends on. Then run a proximity test. Ask: is this scene inside the historic record, adjacent to it, or outside it? If it is inside, verify the source. If it is adjacent, define the gap and make the gap visible to the audience. If it is outside, either cut it or mark it as invention. In a real production, this becomes a simple table: scene, date, fact type, source, risk, and fix. That table is your escape hatch. It stops the edit from becoming a debate about whether a helmet is wrong. It forces the team to ask whether the scene is structurally true. For history enthusiasts, this is the difference between a film that respects the past and a film that merely wears it. The audience does not need every detail perfect. They need the world to behave with consistent rules. When the rules hold, the drama feels earned. When they break, the film feels like a theme park built on a broken map. This is also why Nolan-style historical films work when they compress events but keep causal pressure. They may move a meeting, shorten a war, or merge two characters, but the pressure of the decision remains. The audience forgives compression when the stakes are clear. They do not forgive convenience.

## [04:20] Section 4: Four Ways the Timeline Breaks
- **Type**: `pitfalls` | **Target**: 65s | **Words**: 157

> [!tip] Visual Anchor
> [DIAGRAM: Four Failure Modes: source stacking, decorative accuracy, invisible invention, timeline drift]

The first pitfall is source stacking. You collect ten historians, five books, and a hundred forum threads, then you freeze. The film becomes a research project, not a story. The second pitfall is decorative accuracy. You fix the props but ignore the politics, so the scene looks correct and still feels hollow. The third pitfall is invisible invention. You add a dramatic scene with no source, but you present it as fact, and the audience senses the seam. The fourth pitfall is timeline drift. One scene changes a date, and three later scenes inherit the error. The fix is not more notes. It is one shared timeline, one owner, and one rule: if a fact changes, every dependent scene gets updated. In a history film, the smallest error can become the loudest one, because enthusiasts will find it. But the bigger failure is when the story itself is not true to the human logic of the period.

## [05:25] Section 5: Build the Ledger Before You Shoot
- **Type**: `action_call` | **Target**: 37s | **Words**: 89

> [!tip] Visual Anchor
> [SLIDE: Action: Build the ledger, mark proximity, test human logic]

So here is your move. Build the timeline ledger before you shoot. Mark every scene as inside, adjacent, or outside the historic record. Give each fact a source, a risk, and a fix. Then test the story against human logic, not just costume logic. If a scene survives that test, it is close enough to history to carry the drama. If it does not, cut it, move it, or make the invention visible. History is not a cage. It is a load-bearing wall. Use it, and the film stands.
